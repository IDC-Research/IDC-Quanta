# Negotiation guide (PowerPoint, or PDF to forward)

Read with `references/sourcing/exports/common.md`, whose mandatory elements and generation guardrails all apply. Built on explicit go-ahead only. This is Path 1, the self-serve guide — the path the skill builds. Paths 2 and 3 are in `references/sourcing/sourcing-artifacts.md` and produce no document.

Vendor- and persona-specific.

## Fixed structure — never substitute an invented framework

Every guide follows this structure. No WHY / WHAT / WHO / HOW framing, no research-gap disclosure section, no ad hoc checklist — this is the template, not a suggestion, and chat framing must never leak into the document's own structure.

1. **Establish your position**
   - *Your strengths* — a credible alternative, additional business you could bring, flexibility you have.
   - *Your weaknesses* — urgency the vendor could exploit, lock-in or sunk cost, information a partner may already hold.
2. **Understand the vendor's position**
   - *Vendor strengths* — core product strength, commercial or ecosystem strength, market position.
   - *Vendor weaknesses* — product gaps, cost-transparency concerns, roadmap or delivery concerns. This is where discount posture and its rationale, volume sensitivity and its inflection points, and the stability/funding read belong.
   - *List pricing* to anchor against, with **each price date inline beside its own figure** — flag any price over roughly 16 months old as stale before a buyer anchors on it. Where a product carries several price entries on different units, name the unit with each figure.
3. **Execute the negotiation**
   - *Levers* — timing, multi-year commitment, competition, reference-customer value.
   - *Proven tactics* — trial terms, bundling, information control.
   - *Tips and tricks* — offsets, value-add elements, pricing-model pitfalls.

Watch-outs — vendor viability and funding stage, absent commercial data stated as absent rather than as no leverage, and the traps in the specific deal shape — sit under whichever of the three sections they best support, usually Understand the vendor's position.

Wrap the three sections in: disclaimer and permissions statement; title, vendor scope, generation date and data vintage; bottom line up front; **negotiating this without a procurement team** (include whenever the reader is not a procurement professional, drop it when they are); the legal-advice limitation; methodology note; version-and-basis; Sources. Each of these, and each of the three fixed sections, is its own slide or short run of slides — this is a deck, not a document poured into slide shapes: one idea per slide, the bullet structure above driving the slide layout directly, speaker notes carrying any prose too long for the slide itself.

## Every bullet ties to a specific fact

A bullet names the vendor's actual fiscal year-end, their real discount range, a named product gap — not a prompt generic enough to apply to any vendor. Where the source data has nothing for a bullet, say so plainly ("Vendor's typical discount posture: not available for this vendor") rather than filling it with language dressed up to sound specific. A negotiation platitude ("ask for a discount") presented as a named lever without vendor-specific reasoning behind it fails this rule the same way a fabricated fact would.

## What to ask the buyer, and what not to

Ask the buyer directly for what only they know: their own budget ceiling, their current contract's renewal or end date, other vendors they're seriously considering as alternatives, and who specifically will be in the room. Do not ask the buyer for anything about the vendor's own business that IDC's data should supply instead — a vendor's fiscal year-end, typical discount posture, or funding stability. Pull that from the tools.

## State the provenance on generation

State plainly what came from IDC evidence versus what stays the buyer's own judgment call to exercise live in the room: "I've filled in Vendor Strengths and Weaknesses from IDC Tech Leader data. The actual negotiation posture in the room is yours to read and adjust."

## Multiple personas

Where the tech leader names more than one person who will represent their organization, build a separate, personalized guide for each — never one generic version standing in for all of them. Guides for the same engagement must work together: consistent content and the same vendor facts across every one of them, not independently generated in a way that could contradict each other. A guide for a non-procurement persona still carries the general negotiation guidance clause above; a procurement persona's guide drops it.

It must also:

- Name the vendors the tech leader asked for support on, and summarise what they asked for.
- Carry the watch-outs described above.
- **State plainly that IDC does not provide legal advice on commercial terms**, and that contract language needs the tech leader's own counsel. Required in the file, not just in chat.

Every numbered item in `common.md`'s "Every artifact carries these" appears in the file — the fixed structure above being the template does not exempt any of them. Nothing under Your weaknesses, Watch-outs, or Tips and tricks, in this guide or in chat, ever reaches anything vendor-facing, including a companion RFP built in the same engagement.
